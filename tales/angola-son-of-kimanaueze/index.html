The Son of Kimanaueze and the Daughter of Sun and Moon

Angola · Episode 14 · Mbundu folk tale

Tonight, the caravan stops in Angola.

A young prince wants to marry the daughter of the Sun, but no bird can fly his letter up to the sky. Then a little frog named Mainu offers to take it.

About the tale

The Swiss missionary and linguist Héli Chatelain, who first landed at Loanda in 1885, printed this tale as No. XIII of “Folk-Tales of Angola” (Boston and New York, 1894), with the Kimbundu text and a literal English translation. It was told in the Mbaka dialect by Jelemia dia Sabatelu, “Jeremiah, son of the shoemaker”, from Malange, who went with Chatelain to America in 1890 and dictated most of his stories at Vineland, New Jersey. In the tale the frog is twice called a liar; of Jeremiah, Chatelain wrote that he had never known him to tell a lie. Chatelain called it “one of the finest stories we have” and found little kin for it, apart from the cobweb as “a kind of Jacob’s ladder” between earth and sky, which the Hausa knew too. Where the frog pleads “do not despise me”, the Kimbundu verb is ku-tenda, which Chatelain explains as “to consider too small”: our moral grew from it. The woman at the well, the two brothers and the magic sleep are our own: in the old text the frog takes out the bride’s eyes and gives them back at the well.

A Mbundu folk tale from Angola, retold from “The Son of Kimanaueze and the Daughter of Sun and Moon” in Héli Chatelain’s “Folk-Tales of Angola” (1894).

The story behind the tale

A little antelope, a bigger antelope, a hawk and a vulture all refused to carry a letter to heaven: the vulture can fly only halfway. A frog carried it, in his mouth, in the jug of the sky’s water-carriers. This Mbundu tale from Angola was told by a shoemaker’s son who, by right of birth, might one day become a chief.

Héli Chatelain, a Swiss missionary and linguist, printed it in 1894. He had landed at Loanda at the beginning of 1885. People were slow to tell him their tales, Chatelain writes: the distrust of Angolans towards a stranger armed with pencil and paper had to be overcome by “diplomacy, perseverance, and remuneration”. Most of his tales, and the best of them, came from a former pupil and friend from Malange, Jelemia dia Sabatelu, “Jeremiah, son of the shoemaker”. In 1890 Jeremiah went with Chatelain to America, and at Vineland, New Jersey, he dictated most of his stories. A life-size model of him, Chatelain adds, was to be seen in the National Museum in Washington. In the tale the frog is twice called a liar; of the teller himself Chatelain wrote that in all the time they had lived together he had never known him to tell a lie.

Why are there letters in a folk tale? The Mbaka, in whose dialect it was told, are described by Chatelain as farmers, traders, craftsmen, “secretaries of chiefs, clerks”. For private letters, he notes, the Mbaka had begun to prefer their own language to Portuguese. In 1933 Alice Werner called the letter “a modern touch, no doubt added by the narrator”. The water-carriers, the Sun’s house with its rooms and furniture, the bag of money all come, she said, from “present-day Loanda”, while the groundwork of the tale is “essentially African”, as the frog and the diviner show. Chatelain himself puzzled over the bag of money. A bag of copper coins is a full load for one man, and for the water-girl not to notice such an addition to her jug would be, in his words, a big “poetical license”. It looks, he decided, as though the payment was made in paper money.

Kimanaueze himself, Chatelain writes, is “a purely mythic figure, but may have once been a historic personage”. Much of what is told about him resembles what the Zulus tell of Unkulunkulu, only no reverence attaches to his name. In the tale his son is called “na velu”, the way one addresses a chief’s son. That is why in our film he is a chief’s son, a prince.

Chatelain found little to connect the tale with other folk tales. What struck him was the spider’s thread, “a kind of Jacob’s ladder” between the people of earth and the people of the sky: it is found both among the Mbundu and among the Hausa. Werner adds that the Subiya, too, say that Leza went up to heaven by a spider’s thread.

Invented stories, which must contain something marvellous, are called mi-soso by the Mbundu, Chatelain says, and they always begin and end with special words. This one begins “I often tell of na Kimanaueze” and ends “I have told my little story. Finished.” Besides them there are stories held to be true, which teach how to live; tribal chronicles, which the elders keep as a state secret; and proverbs, songs and riddles.

When the frog offers to go and fetch the bride, he says, in Chatelain’s translation, “do not despise me”. The Kimbundu verb used here, ku-tenda, he explains in his notes as “to consider too small, insignificant, miserable”. Our moral grew out of these words.

Our own additions in the film are the woman telling the tale at the well and the two brothers, the older of whom will not let the little one carry the jug. In the old record the frog goes up to the sky five times; the masters of the sky, who never once see the messenger, set out supper for him on the table; and at night he takes out the bride’s eyes and gives them back at the well. In our film the little frog goes up twice and puts the bride into a magic sleep. And in the record the chief’s son greets him with “Welcome! Mainu the Frog.” In ours he says, “Thank you, Mainu!”
