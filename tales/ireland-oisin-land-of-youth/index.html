Oisín in the Land of Youth

Ireland · Episode 11 · Irish legend

Tonight, the caravan stops in Ireland.

Over the waves comes Niamh on a white horse, to take Oisín to the Land of Youth, where no one grows old. There only three years seem to pass, but he misses his father, Finn, and longs to go home.

About the tale

P. W. Joyce retold this legend in English as “Oisin in Tirnanoge”, the last story in his “Old Celtic Romances” (London, 1879). In Irish it is a poem, which Bryan O’Looney printed with a literal translation in Dublin in 1859; he believed it was written by Michael Comyn about the year 1749, and in it Oisín tells his story to Saint Patrick. Jeremiah Curtin, who took down tales from old storytellers in the west of Ireland in 1887, printed another version in “Myths and Folk-Lore of Ireland” (1890), in which Oisín thinks he has been away “about three years” and his wife answers, “It is three hundred years.” The danger of touching the earth on the way home is much older: in “The Voyage of Bran”, first written down in the seventh century according to Kuno Meyer (1895), one of Bran’s men leaps ashore in Ireland and at once becomes “a heap of ashes”. The old fisherman who tells the tale and the boy who hears it from Oisín are our own.

An Irish legend, retold from “Oisin in Tirnanoge” in P. W. Joyce’s “Old Celtic Romances” (1879) and from the Irish poem “The Land of Youth”, edited by Bryan O’Looney (1859).
