Colapesce, the Boy Who Lived in the Sea

Italy · Episode 7 · Sicilian legend

Tonight, the caravan stops in Italy.

Cola from near Messina swims all day and never comes when his mother calls, until her angry words give him scales and webbed fingers. Then the King throws a golden cup into the sea.

About the tale

A legend of Messina and the Strait of Messina in Sicily. Giuseppe Pitrè collected its Sicilian folk versions and published them in “Studi di leggende popolari in Sicilia e nuova raccolta di leggende siciliane” (Turin: Carlo Clausen, 1904). In Sicilian the hero is Cola Pisci or Piscicola, “Cola the Fish”, and many tellers said that Messina, or all of Sicily, rests on three columns under the sea. Some retellings add that Cola stayed below to hold up the broken column; the folk versions Pitrè recorded only say that he never came back.

A legend from Messina and the Strait of Messina, Sicily, Italy, retold from the Sicilian folk versions collected by Giuseppe Pitrè, “Studi di leggende popolari in Sicilia” (Turin, 1904), chapter “La leggenda di Cola Pesce”.

The story behind the tale

Sicilians used to say that Messina stands on three columns under the sea: one whole, one cracked, one broken. And they would add: “Missina, Missina, un jornu sarai mischina” — “Messina, Messina, one day you will be wretched.” In 1904 the doctor Giuseppe Pitrè printed these words in his study of Cola the Fish. Four years later an earthquake almost levelled Messina.

Pitrè himself took the saying for a later addition: it came from Palermo, Messina’s old rival, and arose no earlier than the 16th century.

Pitrè was born in Palermo in 1841. His father was a sailor, and his mother came from a family of sailors. He worked as a doctor all his life, treating the poor of Palermo during the cholera epidemic, and from sailors and peasants he wrote down songs, proverbs, riddles and tales. He gathered his “Sicilian Folk Songs” partly from his mother’s lips: she, Pitrè said, was his “Library of Sicilian Folk Traditions”. That is also the title of his main work — twenty-five volumes published between 1871 and 1913.

For his study of Cola, Pitrè brought together eighteen folk versions. The first in the book was told in October 1882 by Sara Barbiera, a girl who could not read. He took that one and several others down himself — one of them in 1897 by the lakes of Ganzirri, on a trip to the Faro. Others were sent to him by his brother Antonio and other collectors. Almost everything he recorded himself he printed in the speech of the place where he had heard it, explaining the Sicilian words in footnotes.

Cola is short for Nicola. He nearly always dives off the Faro — and in one version exactly where the currents meet and form a whirlpool. Antonino Grasso of Messina explained that for six hours the current there “rises”, for six it “falls”, and sometimes it twists into a whirlpool. According to Sara Barbiera, there is a cave with fire in it beneath the fort of San Salvatore in Messina. In Grasso’s telling the fire under the sea is like the mouth of Etna.

The legend is far older than Pitrè’s records. In the late 12th century Walter Map wrote of Nicholas Pipe, who lived in the sea for a month, even a whole year, without taking a breath. In the 13th century the friar Salimbene of Parma heard “a hundred and a hundred times” from the friars of Messina how Emperor Frederick II threw a golden cup into the strait and ordered the swimmer to bring it back. The chronicler Francesco Pipino recalled that when he was a child, mothers frightened crying children with Nicola’s name.

In 1797 Schiller turned the story into his ballad “The Diver” (“Der Taucher”), not yet knowing the diver’s name. Zhukovsky translated it into Russian as “The Goblet”: “I throw my golden goblet down there.” In Naples the hero is called Niccolò Pesce: he travels in the bellies of huge fish and gets out by slitting them open with a knife. In Mallorca he is Peix Nicolau.

Pitrè explains why the mother’s angry words came true. Sicilians believed that a mother’s curse comes true because at that moment heaven is open; and if God does not hear her, a passing angel says “Amen” and carries her words to God. In one version the mother then dies of grief. In our film she is only sorry for what she said.

In Roccalumera, on the coast near Messina, Cola is the protector of his fellow townspeople: he stays in the water all the time, watching over the sea monsters so that they cannot harm anyone. The monsters are afraid of him.

Our film is inspired by the Opera dei Pupi, the Sicilian puppet theatre that emerged in the early 19th century. Puppets with wooden frames and real armour act out chivalric tales of Charlemagne’s paladins. In Messina and the east of the island a puppet can stand up to 130 centimetres tall and weigh about 30 kilograms. In 2001 UNESCO proclaimed the puppet theatre a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

And the tales Pitrè collected include the simpleton Giufà, so trusting that he is easy prey for any swindler.
