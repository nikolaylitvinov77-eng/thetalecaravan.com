The Pot That Had a Baby

Turkey · Episode 13 · Turkish folk tale

Tonight, the caravan stops in Turkey.

Nasreddin Hodja borrows a big pot from his neighbour, a coppersmith who loves anything free. He brings it back with a little pot inside: “Your pot had a baby!”

About the tale

“Hodja” is a title, not a name: teacher or scholar. Türkiye’s Ministry of Culture and Tourism says Nasreddin lived from 1208 to 1284 and taught in Akşehir, where his tomb is shown. The jest is in the Turkish folk book of Nasreddin, first printed in Constantinople in 1837; W. B. Barker printed that Turkish text with a word-for-word English translation in London in 1854. In 1878 J.-A. Decourdemanche published it from a manuscript on French paper dated 1757: there Hodja tells his neighbour that he believed the cauldron had given birth when it profited him. George Borrow’s English version (1884) gives the cauldron to a brazier, a worker in brass. According to Albert Wesselski (1911), the jest of the dish that gives birth and then dies was told of the Turkish Nasreddin, the Arab Juha and the Berber Djeha, and on Malta. In 2022 UNESCO inscribed the telling of Nasreddin anecdotes for seven countries, Türkiye among them. The grandmother, the two brothers, the lesson and the ending, where Hodja takes his saucepan back, are our own, and so is the moral.

A Turkish folk tale of Nasreddin Hodja, retold from George Borrow’s “The Turkish Jester” (Ipswich, 1884) and W. B. Barker’s “A Reading Book of the Turkish Language” (London, 1854).

The story behind the tale

Nasreddin Hodja brought back his neighbour’s cauldron with a little saucepan inside and said the cauldron had given birth. The neighbour was glad and kept the saucepan. Then Hodja borrowed the cauldron again; the neighbour waited five days, knocked, and heard that his cauldron had died.

“Hodja” is a title, not a name: it means teacher or scholar. Türkiye’s Ministry of Culture and Tourism says that Nasreddin was born in 1208 in the village of Hortu near Sivrihisar, lived in Akşehir from 1237, was a teacher and a judge there, and died in 1284; the folklorist D. L. Ashliman gives almost the same dates, with the word “reputedly”. In the middle of the seventeenth century the traveller Evliya Çelebi saw Hodja’s tomb in Akşehir. In 1911 Albert Wesselski quoted a description by a Greek writer, Valavanis: a wooden roof on four posts, three sides open to every wind, and on the fourth a locked door with two wooden keys. Wesselski thought it untrue: W. B. Barker had seen there a plain grave with a railing, like the graves around it. In 2026 the ministry’s website says that a big padlock hangs on the door.

Our jest comes from the Turkish folk book of Nasreddin, first printed in Constantinople in 1837. In 1854 Barker published a selection of its stories in London, in Turkish with a word-for-word English translation. When Hodja announces the cauldron’s death, he says “may you remain well”, which, Barker notes, is the phrase of condolence on the death of anyone. In 1878 J.-A. Decourdemanche printed the jest from a manuscript on French paper dated 1757. There Hodja reproaches his neighbour: you believed the cauldron had given birth when it profited you. George Borrow put the jest into English from the Turkish (1884) and gave the cauldron to a brazier, a worker in brass; that is where the coppersmith in our film comes from. In Turkey the jest is remembered: in 2017 the columnist Yusuf Dinç headed a newspaper column “Kazan doğurdu” (“The cauldron gave birth”) and wrote that everyone knows it.

Nasreddin does not belong to one people. Wesselski wrote that in the Caucasus he is a Circassian, in Kazan a Tatar, in Persia a Persian and in Serbia a Serb, while for the Turks he is the folk hero of wit: his jokes were told in coffee houses and during breaks in court hearings. Wesselski found the jest of the vessel that gave birth and then died told of the Turkish Nasreddin, the Arab Juha and the Berber Djeha, and also on Malta. In J. E. Hanauer’s Palestinian version (1907), Juha sells the cauldron in another town and tells its owner that it has died and been devoured by hyenas. In 2022 UNESCO inscribed the tradition of telling Nasreddin anecdotes on its list of intangible heritage for seven countries, among them Türkiye, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan.

In the Aarne–Thompson index of tale types our jest is number 1592B, and 1592 is “The Iron-Eating Mice”. That story is in the Indian Panchatantra: a merchant left an iron balance-beam in pledge, and when he came back he was told that mice had eaten it. He did not argue. He led away the keeper’s son and said that a hawk had carried the boy off. The judges heard them both, laughed, and told each to give back what belonged to the other. The same plot is in La Fontaine and in Leo Tolstoy’s fable “The Two Merchants”.

A Turkish proverb says “Bedava sirke baldan tatlıdır”: free vinegar is sweeter than honey. And a collection of Turkish proverbs in English, printed in 1880 at the Armenian monastery of St Lazarus in Venice, has “Even pitch is welcome if given gratis” and “The kettle calls the saucepan smutty”.

Our own additions in the film are the grandmother with the two brothers and the lesson for the neighbour. The folk book does not say why Hodja started it all, and the story ends with his question; in our film Hodja returns the cauldron and takes back his own saucepan. The moral at the end is ours too.
