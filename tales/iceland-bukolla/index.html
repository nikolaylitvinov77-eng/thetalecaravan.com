Búkolla, the Magic Cow

Iceland · Episode 4 · Icelandic folk tale

Tonight, the caravan stops in Iceland.

A boy searches for his family’s lost cow, and three hairs from her tail become a river, a fire and a mountain.

About the tale

Jón Árnason collected Icelandic folk legends and tales and published them in two large volumes in 1862–1864. In the version a woman from Seltjarnarnes, near Reykjavík, told to the collectors, the giantess gets stuck in the mountain and turns to stone. In another version, printed in the full edition of the collection (1954–1961), the trolls turn to stone when daylight shines over the sea. Throwing things behind you to stop a chase is known in many lands, from Japan and India to Europe.

An Icelandic folk tale, retold from “Búkolla og strákurinn” (printed as “Búkolla” in Jón Árnason’s “Íslenzkar þjóðsögur og æfintýri”, 1862–1864) and “Búkollu saga” from the full edition (1954–1961).

The story behind the tale

An Icelandic legend says that the island of Drangey, off the north coast, was once a cow. Two night trolls were leading her across the fjord and did not make it before dawn.

“Búkolla” is another story about a cow and trolls, only with a happy ending. An old couple own one cow, “and that was all their livestock”, and a son they do not love. When the cow vanishes, they send the boy to find her with food for the road and new shoes: he is not to come back without Búkolla.

He calls her at every stop, and she answers right under his feet, from a giantess’s cave. He leads the cow away, the giantess gives chase, and Búkolla tells him to pull a hair from her tail and speaks a spell over it: become a river so wide that no one can cross it “but a flying bird”. A bull is brought to the giantess and drinks the river dry. The second hair becomes a fire, which the bull puts out with the water it drank; the third, a mountain up to the sky. The giantess drills through, squeezes into the hole too soon, gets stuck and turns to stone, and the boy brings Búkolla home.

In our film we softened a few things. Nobody drives the son away: he sets out on his own, and his mother gets him ready for the road. And the bull puts out the fire by spitting out the river — in one recorded version, the giantess’s dogs do this.

Our film follows the version Magnús Grímsson wrote down from a woman at Seltjarnarnes, near Reykjavík. Magnús and his friend Jón Árnason, a librarian, collected folk stories inspired by the Brothers Grimm. In 1858 the German legal historian Konrad Maurer toured Iceland, wrote down legends himself and urged the two friends to keep going.

With Maurer’s help, two volumes came out in Leipzig in 1862 and 1864. The collectors had neither the time nor the means to travel much, so stories reached them by letter from all over the country, mostly from clergymen.

In old Iceland value in barter was counted in cows: one kúgildi, or “cow value”, equalled six woolly pregnant sheep. One version of “Búkolla” says plainly that there was nothing to eat in the house but Búkolla’s milk.

In Icelandic legends night trolls turn to stone in daylight, and so it was at Drangey: the cow became the island, and the trolls two stone pillars in the sea. The southern one, the Old Hag, still stands; the northern one, the Old Man, collapsed as long ago as the eighteenth century.

The outlaw Grettir the Strong, hero of “Grettis saga”, one of the sagas of Icelanders written down in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, spent his last three years on that same island. He was killed there, by the usual dating, in 1031.

In Jón Árnason’s collection “Búkolla” stands among the fairy tales, beside stories of ghosts, sorcerers, outlaws and saints, and of the hidden people who live in hills and rocks. Belief in invisible neighbours has not quite gone: in 2013 protests by environmental groups and elf supporters halted work on a road near Reykjavík for a time.

Jón Árnason marked the scene where the giantess drills through the mountain with a footnote: “compare the Edda”. In the Younger Edda the giant Baugi drills through a mountain for Odin, who slips through the hole as a snake to reach the mead of poetry. The giantess in “Búkolla” was less lucky.

Things thrown behind them save runaways in many lands. In the Japanese chronicle “Kojiki” (around 712), the god Izanagi, fleeing the land of the dead, throws the vine from his hair and his comb at his pursuers, and they turn into grapes and bamboo shoots. In Afanasyev’s collection of Russian tales, runaways escape Baba Yaga the same way: a towel becomes a river, a comb a forest.

In Russian tales, too, a cow comes to a child’s rescue — in “Kroshechka-Khavroshechka”: “Climb into one of my ears and out of the other, and all the work will be done.”
