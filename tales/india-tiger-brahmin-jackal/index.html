The Tiger, the Brahmin and the Jackal

India · Episode 3 · folk tale from the Punjab

Tonight, the caravan stops in India.

A Brahmin lets a tiger out of its cage, the tiger wants to eat him, and only a clever jackal can settle the case.

About the tale

Flora Annie Steel heard this tale from a village boy of the Jatt farming caste in the Gujranwala district of the Punjab (today in Pakistan); it was also heard in the Firozpur and Lahore districts. The journal “Indian Antiquary” printed it in 1883, and in 1894 it went into her book “Tales of the Punjab”, illustrated by John Lockwood Kipling, the father of Rudyard Kipling. Nineteenth-century scholars gathered about a hundred versions of the story, from India to Europe. In a twelfth-century Latin book by Petrus Alphonsi the judge is a fox, and in a Russian tale from Afanasyev’s collection an old mare, an old dog and a fox are asked to judge.

A folk tale from the Punjab, retold from “The Tiger, the Brahman, and the Jackal” in Flora Annie Steel’s “Tales of the Punjab” (1894).

The story behind the tale

A tiger in a cage swears to a Brahmin: let me out, and “I should be for ever grateful, and serve you as a slave!” The Brahmin opens the door, and the tiger at once seizes him to eat him. Afanasyev’s collection of Russian tales has the same story, only with a wolf, and a fox settles the dispute.

Our caravan has stopped in the Punjab of British India, and the cartoon follows “The Tiger, the Brahman, and the Jackal”, written down by Flora Annie Steel, an Englishwoman married to a colonial official. She lived in India until 1889, chiefly in the Punjab, and in winter toured the districts her husband served in, asking village children for stories. Every village, the book’s preface says, has “a story-teller par excellence”, “generally a boy”. This tale came from a boy of the Jatt farming caste in the Gujranwala district, now in Pakistan.

In June 1883 the journal “Indian Antiquary” printed it. There the jackal tells the Brahmin he looks “as miserable as a Jatt in a shower”, a well-known Punjabi proverb. In the notes to the 1894 book R. C. Temple wrote that anyone would understand it who had seen a Punjabi farmer heading to his field “on a wet, bleak February morning, with his scant clothing sticking to his limp and shivering figure”. Yet in that book, “Tales of the Punjab” (1894), illustrated by John Lockwood Kipling, Rudyard Kipling’s father, the proverb has turned into one any English reader would grasp: “as miserable as a fish out of water”.

The tiger’s rescuer is a Brahmin, a member of the highest varna, or class, of Hindu society. Brahmins were priests and teachers, and later many became judges and officials. Yet this one has to find his own judges: as agreed with the tiger, he asks the first three things he comes across. In 1859 the German Sanskrit scholar Theodor Benfey called this custom typically Indian.

Real village disputes went to the panchayat, literally the “assembly of five”, a council of respected elders. In the tale the elders are replaced by a pipal, the sacred fig, whose branches people tear down to feed their cattle; a buffalo turning a well-wheel; and the road itself, repaid with “the ashes of their pipes”. All have been badly used by people, and none takes the Brahmin’s side. The jackal saves him by playing dumb until the tiger jumps back into the cage to show how it was, then shuts the door. In our cartoon the road stays only in the background.

Jackals were plentiful around Gujranwala: the district gazetteer of 1883–84 reports “hares and jackals in abundance”, but names no tigers among the local animals. In Indian fables the jackal is an old trickster. In the first book of the Panchatantra, a Sanskrit collection of fables, the jackal Damanaka sets the lion king against his friend the bull. Through the Arabic “Kalila and Dimna” and an eleventh-century Greek tale, these fables reached the Slavs as “Stephanites and Ichnelates”.

The story has travelled far: in 1891 the Finnish folklorist Kaarle Krohn examined 94 versions of it. In the southern Panchatantra a Brahmin, out of pity, carries a crocodile to the Ganges in his travelling bag, and the fox who judges the case lures it back into the bag. In Petrus Alphonsi’s twelfth-century Latin book a fox settles a dispute between a man and a snake. In 1868 Mary Frere printed a tale from southern India in “Old Deccan Days”: the Brahmin goes round six judges, the last a jackal he calls “Mama”, meaning “uncle”.

In the Russian tale “Old Bread and Salt Is Forgotten” a peasant hides a wolf from hunters in his sack, and once out, the wolf threatens to eat him. An old mare and an old dog, both cast off by their masters, side with the wolf. The fox tricks the wolf back into the sack, and the peasant beats it with his flail. The ending is more bitter than the Indian one: the peasant kills the fox who saved him, saying, “Old bread and salt is forgotten!” — past kindness counts for nothing.
