The Troll Who Built Kalundborg Church

Denmark · Episode 6 · Danish legend

Tonight, the caravan stops in Denmark.

Esbern wants to build a church but has too little money, so a troll offers to build it. When the work is done, Esbern must guess the troll’s name, or he will belong to the troll.

About the tale

A Danish legend from the town of Kalundborg on the island of Zealand; the man who wanted to build the church is Esbern Snare, and the troll’s name is Fin. Thomas Keightley retold it as “Kallundborg Church” in “The Fairy Mythology” (new edition, London: George Bell & Sons, 1892), after Thiele’s “Danmarks Folkesagn”. The legend says the church got only three and a half pillars; in fact four granite columns hold up its central tower, and the real church has five towers.

A legend from Kalundborg, Zealand, Denmark, retold from Thomas Keightley, “The Fairy Mythology” (1892), after Thiele’s “Danmarks Folkesagn”.

The story behind the tale

In the Danish town of Kalundborg, a church with five towers stands on a hill above the harbour — the only church of its kind in Denmark. Local legend says a troll built it, and the man who wanted it had to guess the troll’s name or lose his heart and eyes.

That man was real. Esbern Snare (1127–1204) was a nobleman of Zealand from the powerful Hvide clan and the elder brother of Absalon, who would become Archbishop of Lund. The brothers grew up together with Prince Valdemar, the future King Valdemar I. Around 1170 Esbern built a castle by the natural harbour, a town grew up around it, and he is considered the founder of Kalundborg.

In the legend Esbern has too little money for the church. According to Danish sources, he laid its foundations, but it was probably finished after his death by his daughter Ingeborg and her husband. The church is laid out as a Greek cross with four arms of equal length. Each arm ends in an octagonal tower 34 metres tall, and in the middle rises a square tower of 44 metres. The five towers were meant to recall the Heavenly Jerusalem, which the Middle Ages imagined as a fortified city with five towers. The central tower rests on four granite columns. In 1827 it collapsed — no one was hurt — and by 1871 it had been rebuilt.

The legend was written down by Just Mathias Thiele, who worked at the Royal Library in Copenhagen. Following the example of the Brothers Grimm, he travelled the country collecting legends, both from written sources and from what people told him. A legend Thiele marks “oral” is one he heard himself from country people; “communicated” means friends sent it to him. The Kalundborg legend is oral. Thiele first printed it as early as 1818–1823, and in his expanded collection of 1843 it stands among the legends of churches and monasteries.

Thiele was one of Hans Christian Andersen’s early benefactors, and it was for Thiele’s daughter Ida that Andersen wrote “Little Ida’s Flowers”. Andersen himself loved cutting paper while children listened to his stories, and our film’s look is inspired by his paper cuts.

The legend was retold in English by the Irishman Thomas Keightley in “The Fairy Mythology”. In his version it explains “the reason that the church has but three pillars and a half”, while a note adds: “Mr. Thiele says he saw four pillars at the church.”

A similar story was told of the cathedral at Lund — Danish then, Swedish now. There the giant is called Finn. In the cathedral’s crypt stand two stone figures, and the larger one embraces a column. They are traditionally called “The giant Finn and his wife”, though most scholars today believe the larger figure depicts the biblical Samson.

Keightley compared the Danish legend with the Brothers Grimm’s “Rumpelstiltskin”. Folklorists group tales in which guessing a helper’s name saves the day into a single type, “The Name of the Supernatural Helper”. It includes the English “Tom Tit Tot” and the Icelandic “Gilitrutt”, and according to scholarship the story is especially popular in Denmark, Finland, Germany and Ireland.

In 1865 the American poet John Greenleaf Whittier published a ballad about Kalundborg, “Kallundborg Church”. In his version Esbern builds the church to win the hand of the fair Helva.

Churches and giants are a theme of their own in Danish legend. Evald Tang Kristensen, a schoolteacher from Jutland, gathered material from some 6,500 storytellers: about 2,700 fairy tales and 25,000 legends, sayings, poems and riddles. One of his volumes is simply titled “Giants. Churches. Other Local Legends. Treasures.”

And every farm, folk belief held, had its own nisse — a house spirit dressed in grey with a pointed red cap, no taller than a ten-year-old boy. At night he was left a bowl of porridge with butter.

In our film the troll asks for neither heart nor eyes: he threatens to take Esbern himself as a toy for his troll child.
