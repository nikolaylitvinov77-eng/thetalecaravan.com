The Badger Tea-Kettle

Japan · Episode 2 · Japanese folk tale

Also known as: Bunbuku Chagama

Tonight, the caravan stops in Japan.

A temple kettle turns out to be a badger, a poor tinker is kind to it, and the badger decides to repay him.

About the tale

This tale has a real address: Morinji, a Zen temple in Tatebayashi, founded in 1426, which still shows its famous kettle to visitors in its treasure hall. All over Japan the folk tale tells of a grateful animal that turns into a kettle to help a poor man. A children’s picture book about it appeared in the Edo period, and in 1895 Iwaya Sazanami retold it in his series “Japanese Old Tales”. In most Japanese retellings the hero is a tanuki, a raccoon dog; A. B. Mitford, who retold the story in English in 1871, called it a badger.

A Japanese folk tale set at the Morinji temple, retold from “The Accomplished and Lucky Tea-Kettle” in A. B. Mitford’s “Tales of Old Japan” (1871) and “The Tea-Kettle” in Grace James’s “Green Willow and Other Japanese Fairy Tales” (1910).

The story behind the tale

You can see the badger tea-kettle with your own eyes: it lives in a Japanese temple, and a ticket costs 300 yen. And the monk who brought it there, legend says, once fell sound asleep — and grew fur and a tail.

Morinji, a Zen temple in Tatebayashi, dates its history to 1426, so in 2026 it turns six hundred. By its legend the monk Shukaku came with the founder, and in 1570, when crowds gathered for a great service, he brought a kettle that never ran dry. He named it the “happiness-sharing” kettle and promised eight blessings to all who drank from it. Under the tenth abbot the secret came out: Shukaku was a mujina, a magic shape-shifting beast. He left in 1587, and the temple still honours him as its guardian.

Here the shape-shifter is the monk himself. In the akahon, thin “red books” for children of the mid-Edo period, about ten pages long, the animal becomes the kettle. In one, a shaven-headed tea servant of the retired shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa catches a mujina for soup; it plays dead and turns into a kettle, but over the fire it cannot bear the heat and gives itself away. In 1895 the writer Iwaya Sazanami published his own “Bunbuku Chagama”. According to the temple, the local legend became Iwaya’s fairy tale, and the tanuki walking a tightrope, head and paws poking out of a kettle, has been famous ever since.

A. B. Mitford joined the British Legation in Japan in 1866. For “Tales of Old Japan” (1871) he found the children’s stories in little illustrated pamphlets printed from blocks so worn the text was hardly legible. “These are the first tales which are put into a Japanese child’s hands,” he wrote. The Japanese, he noted, believed badgers, foxes and cats could take human shape and, like Western fairies, “work for good as well as for evil ends”. In the very next tale of the book, “The Crackling Mountain” (“Kachi-kachi Yama”), the badger is a villain who kills an old woman.

Grace James, born in Tokyo in 1882, lived in Japan until she was twelve. Many tales in her “Green Willow” (1910), she wrote, “are told from memory, being relics of childish days, originally heard from the lips of a school-fellow or a nurse”. Her kettle introduces itself — “My name is Bumbuku-Chagama” — and at the end asks to be given to the temple of Morinji. The night scene at the tinker’s and the farewell in our film come from her.

The animal itself is a tanuki, a raccoon dog. The Japanese word mujina means a badger but was used for the tanuki too, and Russian folklorists traditionally call it a badger as well. Folk belief says the tanuki, like the fox, tricks people and turns into a person or a thing. At the Shojoji temple in Kisarazu they tell how one autumn night the abbot and a band of tanuki held a music contest, and in the morning a big tanuki was found that had beaten time on its belly until it burst. With the Morinji legend, this is one of Japan’s three great tanuki legends.

Japanese folklorists distinguish mukashi-banashi, invented tales that begin “mukashi mukashi” (“long, long ago”), from densetsu, legends with an address, meant to be believed. The kettle lives in both. All over Japan people tell of a fox or a tanuki that turns into a kettle out of gratitude to a poor man, so that he can sell it. The folklorist Ozawa Toshio sees in this a comic echo of the tale of the magician and his pupil, which Afanasyev’s Russian collection calls “The Crafty Science” (“Khitraya nauka”): a son turns into a horse, and his father sells him at the fair.

In Mitford and James the kettle is bought by a tinker, a travelling mender of metal pots. Edo-period Japan had its own, the ikakeya, who walked the town with boxes on a long carrying pole, patching copper and iron pots. Our English film keeps the tinker; the Russian version makes him a junk dealer.

More than twenty tanuki statues still line the path to Morinji, and many shopkeepers put a ceramic tanuki by the door for luck: the word tanuki also suggests the meaning “to surpass others”.
