Why the Sun and the Moon Live in the Sky

Nigeria · Episode 10 · Nigerian folk tale

Tonight, the caravan stops in Nigeria.

Long ago the Sun and the Water were great friends on the earth. The Water will not visit: its fish and crabs would leave no room. So the Sun and the Moon build a huge house for their friend.

About the tale

Elphinstone Dayrell, District Commissioner in Southern Nigeria, published this tale in London in 1910, in his book “Folk Stories from Southern Nigeria, West Africa”. He does not say who told it to him; other tales in the same book mention Calabar and the Efik and Ibibio peoples. Andrew Lang, who wrote the introduction, noted that this explanation of why the Sun and the Moon left the earth was, as far as he knew, “without parallel elsewhere”. In 1968 Houghton Mifflin published the tale as a picture book illustrated by Blair Lent, and in 1969 the book received a Caldecott Honor for Lent’s pictures. A Nigerian folk tale, retold from “Why the Sun and the Moon live in the Sky” in Elphinstone Dayrell’s “Folk Stories from Southern Nigeria, West Africa” (1910).

The story behind the tale

The Sun and the Moon live in the sky because they once invited the Water to visit. In 1910 Andrew Lang wrote that this Nigerian explanation of their retreat to the sky was, as far as he knew, “without parallel elsewhere”.

The tale was written down by Elphinstone Dayrell, a district commissioner in Southern Nigeria, in “Folk Stories from Southern Nigeria, West Africa” (London, 1910), with an introduction by Lang. The book holds forty stories; ours is the sixteenth.

The book does not say who told it to Dayrell: for this tale he names neither the teller nor the teller’s people, and he wrote no preface of his own. In his 1913 collection Dayrell noted that every story there came through an interpreter, often two. The grandmother who tells the tale in our film is our own invention.

Later retellings call it an Efik tale. In 1968 Dayrell’s text became an American picture book, and Blair Lent’s pictures for it won a Caldecott Honor. Wikipedia says a version of the story has long been told by coastal peoples of West Africa, among them speakers of Efik-Ibibio, and the Smithsonian Libraries call it a tale of the Efik people of south-eastern Nigeria.

Dayrell’s Water is “he”, with “his people”. He warns at once that his friend must build a very large compound, “a tremendous place, as my people are very numerous”. He flows in “accompanied by the fish and all the water animals”, and when he asks, “Do you want more of my people to come?”, the Sun and the Moon both answer yes, “not knowing any better”.

Hugh Goldie, a missionary, shows what such a compound might have been like. In 1890 he described the houses of Calabar: windowless rooms stood round a court or yard and shared one entrance from the street; “court can be added to court”, and a hundred or more people might live in one house, each man “his own house-builder”.

Goldie also compiled a dictionary of the Efik language, and in 1865 Richard Burton reprinted proverbs from it. Two sound almost as if they were about this tale. “That man makes his house big” means he grows wealthy: a grand abode, the book explains, is the highest ambition of a “gentleman”. And “Water rests in the place it should rest”: everything according to its nature. Of a moon that does not shine at suppertime, the Efik said, “The moon refuses food.”

Dayrell’s book has another tale of how the Moon left the earth for good. There she is a fat, round woman who often comes down to earth. Out of pity she lets a starving old woman cut a little of her meat away every evening, and so grows smaller. One day the people rush out shouting, and the frightened Moon goes back to the sky, never to come down again.

In our tale the Moon is the Sun’s wife, and among the Ibibio, as Percy Amaury Talbot writes in his 1923 book, people said much the same. Chief Inaw of Ikotobo told him that at dawn the full moon “is following her husband the sun. All the small stars are the children of the sun and moon.”

Dayrell’s record ends with the Sun and the Moon staying in the sky. The Sun’s evening visits to the Water are our addition, prompted by another chief in Talbot’s book: “When the sun goes down, it sinks into the big water. We are lost in wonder and cannot tell how it gets round to rise in the East!” He judged the sun to be “about the size of a big plate”. The moral at the end of our film is ours too: Dayrell gives none.

Two years after Dayrell’s book, Talbot published a tale of the Ekoi people, “How Sun and Moon went up to the Sky”, told by Ite Okonni of Aking. There the Sun and the Moon are brothers. Their father turns against the Sun, who is too hot and burns up the young plants in the farms, and praises the Moon for shining softly at night. Fearing their father’s disfavour, both go up to the sky, and he is left on earth with his third son, Darkness.
