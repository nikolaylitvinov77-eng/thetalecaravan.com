Unamir and the Maiden of the Seventh Heaven

Morocco · Episode 8 · Amazigh folk tale

Tonight, the caravan stops in Morocco.

Each morning Unamir wakes with henna on his hands. He catches one of the sky maidens, and she agrees to be his wife if he builds her seven rooms under one key and lets no one in.

About the tale

A tale of the Shilha (Amazigh, Berber) people of Tazerwalt in southern Morocco, who speak Tashelhit. Hans Stumme published it in German translation, with the Shilha text, in “Märchen der Schluḥ von Tázerwalt” (Leipzig: J. C. Hinrichs, 1895), No. 10, “Die Geschichte von Achmed Unamir”. In the old telling the ending is harsher; we kept its heart: a son’s love for his mother, and the five springs.

A tale of the Shilha (Amazigh, Berber) people of Tazerwalt in southern Morocco, retold from Hans Stumme, “Märchen der Schluḥ von Tázerwalt” (Leipzig, 1895), No. 10, “Die Geschichte von Achmed Unamir”.

The story behind the tale

This tale of a young man who caught a sky maiden was written down by a German scholar from the dictation of a Moroccan — the director of a troupe of travelling acrobats. In May 1894 the troupe was performing in Stockholm; that autumn, Shilha acrobats came to the fair in Leipzig.

The linguist Hans Stumme had studied Shilha since 1889, first in Tunis, then in Leipzig, Berlin and Dresden. Every tale in his 1895 collection was dictated by one man: Hajj Abdulla Ben Mhammed of Tazerwalt in southern Morocco. Stumme said he always wrote “as I believed I heard during the dictation”. To the texts of his collection he added a word-for-word German translation. His notes show his doubts. At the end of this tale five springs burst from a rock, and in a note Stumme asks whether “five springs” might be a slip in the dictation — perhaps there were ten?

These troupes did more than tumble. Stumme points out that acrobatics were only one part of the show, alongside singing, and not always the main part.

In Tazerwalt, Stumme wrote, lies the tomb of Sidi Ahmed ou Moussa, patron of the “semi-religious brotherhood of Moroccan acrobats”. This Sufi saint lived in the 15th and 16th centuries, and Moroccan acrobats are still called his children.

In the record, Unamir’s teacher beats him every morning: the boy has henna on his hands, and henna, Stumme explains, is a custom of women and small children, not fitting for a schoolboy. Our film has no teacher — Unamir himself hides a lit candle in a pot.

The tale in our film is unlocked by the keeper of an agadir. An agadir is a fortified communal granary of the Amazigh villages of the Anti-Atlas and the High Atlas; some are more than a thousand years old. Inside are rows of cell-like rooms, the upper ones reached by stones jutting out of the wall, and each family had its own place. People kept grain there, and money, jewellery and papers. The word agadir came from the Phoenicians and means “wall” — which is where the city of Agadir got its name.

Stumme also saw in the tale the “Hasan type”, after the story of Hasan of Basra. In the Thousand and One Nights, Hasan, too, marries a bird maiden. Years later she gets hold of her hidden feathers by a trick, flies off while her husband is away, and leaves word with his mother where to look for her. W. A. Clouston, whom Stumme cites, compared the tale’s forbidden door with “the ‘Blue Chamber’ in our nursery tale of the terrible Bluebeard” and with an Eastern story in which a young man opens a forbidden door and a huge eagle carries him away from his happy life. And in the Russian tale “The Sea Tsar and Vasilisa the Wise”, the Sea Tsar’s daughters fly down to bathe as doves in some records and as spoonbills in others.

In Morocco a similar story is also known as Hammou Ounamir, and it was made into a TV series in the Amazigh language. Tashelhit, the language of the Shilha, was spoken by 4.7 million people in the 2014 census — 14% of Morocco’s population.

Besides wonder tales, Stumme’s book holds legends, comic stories, animal tales, fables and 27 riddles. One fable explains why the sea is salty. Once the sea was fresh, and it wanted to swallow the whole world. To humble its pride, God told a mosquito to drink the sea. The mosquito drank until it reached the sand, then spat the water back out — and the sea has been salty ever since, because it has been inside a mosquito.

The riddles are short. “Two little horses travel all day and never tire”: the eyes. “A tray of eggs turned upside down, yet the eggs don’t scatter”: the stars. “A grain of wheat that fills a whole room”: the flame of a lamp. If nobody guesses, the riddler gives the answer — politely: “It will probably be…”
