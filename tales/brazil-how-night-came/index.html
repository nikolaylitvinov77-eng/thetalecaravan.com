How Night Came

Brazil · Episode 12 · Brazilian myth

Tonight, the caravan stops in Brazil.

Long ago there was only day, and night slept under the water with the Great Snake. The Snake’s daughter cannot sleep without night, so her husband sends three servants to fetch it.

About the tale

Couto de Magalhães, who by his own count governed the provinces of Goiás, Pará and Mato Grosso for about six years, printed this myth as the first legend in “O Selvagem” (Rio de Janeiro, 1876), a course of Nheengatu, “the good language”, printed by order of the government. He gave it in Nheengatu with a word-for-word Portuguese translation, as tapuios, Indians long converted to Christianity, told it, and noted that where the night sings inside the nut, the storytellers imitate the buzzing of night insects. The two birds have names: the cujubim sings when morning breaks, and by the song of the inambu, Magalhães wrote, Indians told the hours of the night. Hartley Burr Alexander (1920) called it “among the most interesting of all American myths”, one that begins “with an illuminated world rather than a chaotic gloom”. In 1917 Elsie Spicer Eells retold it in English as “How Night Came”. The mother who tells the tale, the oldest servant who says no (in 1876 it is the helmsman) and the moral at the end are our own.

A Brazilian myth told in Nheengatu, retold from “Como a noite appareceu” in Couto de Magalhães’s “O Selvagem” (Rio de Janeiro, 1876).

The story behind the tale

In this tale night is carried home in a sealed nut of the tucumã palm and opened on the way by those who want to know what is singing inside. It was written down by the author of a textbook for army interpreters, who admitted that his Karajá boatmen knew the stars better than he did.

Couto de Magalhães’s book “O Selvagem” came out in Rio de Janeiro by order of the government; its preface is dated 2 January 1876. By his own account, Magalhães had governed the provinces of Goiás, Pará and Mato Grosso for about six years, and he proposed to teach the Indians Portuguese through army interpreters; according to the book’s epigraph, this meant conquering two thirds of the country’s territory. The first part of the book is a course in the “general language”, Nheengatu. The word means “the good language”: an Indian on the Amazon, Magalhães wrote, would not understand if you asked him to speak Tupi; you had to ask him to speak the good language.

He wrote the legends down in the backlands of Brazil as he heard them from tapuios, his word for baptized Indians and their descendants. “How Night Came” stands first: a line in Nheengatu, and under it a word-for-word Portuguese translation. The language, not the legends, was the main thing in the book, he said. Where the night sings inside the nut, “ten, ten, ten… xi…”, the storytellers buzzed like night insects. Magalhães supposed that the myth was a fragment of a Genesis of the ancient South American Indians: strip it of the coarse clothes in which grandmothers and wet nurses had probably dressed it, and you see that people everywhere have asked where we came from.

In the old record the birds have names. The daughter of the Great Snake (Cobra Grande in the Portuguese) winds up a thread and says, “You shall be the cujubim”; she whitens its head with clay and paints its legs with red urucum, and it sings when morning breaks. The second thread she sprinkles with ash: the inambu will sing in the evening, at night, at midnight, in the deep night and at dawn. Indians, Magalhães writes elsewhere in the book, divided day and night into stretches of two or three hours, and at night told them by the stars, the moon and the song of the inambu, as Christians tell them by the cock. He travelled dozens, perhaps hundreds of nights on the Araguaia River with crews of Karajá Indians. They always knew the hour by the stars, and Magalhães was not ashamed to say that he knew far fewer constellations than they did. Once they showed him a patch of sky near the Southern Cross: the head of a rhea, the great flightless bird of the plains. As the night went on, its neck and body showed more and more clearly along the Milky Way.

In 1920 Hartley Burr Alexander placed the myth “among the most interesting of all American myths”, one that begins “with an illuminated world rather than a chaotic gloom”. According to the motif catalogue of Yuri Berezkin and Evgeny Duvakin, night carried in a vessel or a bundle is known in Melanesia, in North America and among many peoples of Amazonia. Among the Makú, an older brother opens on the way a little box from the chief of the crickets. On the Banks Islands, R. H. Codrington recorded, the hero Qat sails with a bound pig in his canoe to where night is, buys night and comes home “with a fowl and other birds, to give notice of the time for the return of light”.

Monkeys with black mouths turn up in other Amazonian stories too. In João Barbosa Rodrigues’s collection “Poranduba amazonense” they shiver in a downpour, promise to build a house tomorrow and forget it by morning. Hence the Indian saying “so say the monkeys”, about a promise that will never be kept.

In 1917 Elsie Spicer Eells retold the myth in English as “How Night Came”: her version has a Great Sea Serpent, a bag, three slaves and a cock as the watchman of the night. Our own additions in the film are the mother who tells the tale, the oldest servant in place of the helmsman, and the moral at the end. In the old record the Snake’s daughter will not lie with her husband while it is light, because it is not yet night; in our film, as in Eells, she simply cannot sleep in the light.
